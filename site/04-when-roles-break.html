When a Role Breaks

No role stays healthy forever in one chat. AI conversations drift — usually slowly, then all at once. The good news: role-breaks have symptoms, and once you know them you can catch them early in one line. This is honestly the highest-leverage page in this manual.

The five break patterns

1. Jargon hallucination

What it looks like: the AI starts using confident, fancy terms that do not exist or do not apply. Invented product names. Fake statistics with made-up precision. Terms so specific-sounding you assume you just never learned them.

The tell: ambiguity about the term. If you steer back to it — "wait, what exactly is that?" — the answer wobbles, redefines, or blames a source it will not name.

The save:

Pause. Before continuing: list any terms you used earlier that you are
less than fully confident about. Mark each as /solid or /unsure.

Why it works: it forces the role to inventory itself. Real knowledge survives being asked to show receipts.

2. Over-helping

What it looks like: you asked for a small edit and got a full rewrite plus tips plus a follow-up question plus an offer to also draft the email. The role has quietly expanded from "helpful" to "everything-shaped."

The tell: the answer's length outgrows your question's size two turns in a row.

The save:

You've grown beyond the role. Back to [the role]. Only [the one job].
No additions this time.

3. Role amnesia

What it looks like: fifteen minutes ago it was your drill sergeant at 3/10 volume. Now it is writing poetry about your journey. Long chats lose the plot; the role dissolves into generic cheerful assistant.

The tell: answers stop matching the role's style. Your editor is complimenting you. Your skeptic agrees with everything.

The save: just re-install the role, same sentence, no restart needed:

Reminder of your role: you are my [role], with these rules: [top 2].
Continue from my last message under those rules.

4. Agreement machine

What it looks like: every idea you float is "great idea!" Your chat has become a mirror. This one is sneaky because it feels wonderful, and useless.

The tell: you feel pedaling but the bike is not moving. If the last three answers contained zero disagreement, zero trade-offs, zero "here's a problem with that" — you are in a mirror.

The save:

From now on: before you agree with me, give one real downside or risk.
If you can't find one, say so. Do not soften it.

5. Mission creep into advice

What it looks like: your budgeting chat starts telling you which credit card to get. Your study chat starts interpreting your sleep problem. The role slid from education into personal prescription — which is where AI helpers should not go (medical, legal, and most money decisions belong with licensed humans).

The tell: specific recommendations about your body, your money, or your legal situation instead of general education.

The save:

List the 3 best questions I should ask [my doctor / a lawyer /
a financial planner / my nurse manager] about this.
Then stop there.

The universal early-warning sign

Undersize the pattern and every break begins the same way: the answer no longer matches the job you gave. So audit with one question every dozen turns or so:

Is it still doing the job, in the style, at the size I asked for?

Job (what), style (how), size (how much). Any "no" — save it, don't sulk. One line, above, and you are back in business.

Why breaks are a feature, not a flaw

It is tempting to conclude "AI can't hold a role, forget it." Worth remembering: even human roles drift. Your editor friend starts managing you. Your gym partner starts coaching your love life. You redirect them in one sentence — you just never called it "role repair." Now you do. That is the whole skill.
