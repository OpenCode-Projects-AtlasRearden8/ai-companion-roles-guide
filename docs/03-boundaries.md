# Setting Boundaries in Prompts

Roles tell the AI *who to be*. Boundaries tell it *how far to go*. Skip the role and you get guesswork. Skip the boundaries and you get a wonderful answer to the wrong question at 900 words when you had 40 seconds.

## Why beginners under-boundary

Two honest reasons:

1. **Politeness guilt.** It feels rude to say "no lists," "under 150 words," "do not add tips." But the AI has no feelings — the boundary is purely for *you*. Every boundary you set is a gift to your future self who does not have to skim a wall of text.
2. **Not knowing what to bound.** So start with the big four, below.

## The Big Four boundaries

Almost every prompt gets better with at least two of these:

**Length.** Say a number. "Under 100 words." "Five sentences max." "One paragraph." Numbers beat vibes, always.

**Format.** Say the shape. "As a numbered list." "As one message I can copy-paste." "As a table with two columns: step / who does it." Format is where copy-paste usefulness lives.

**Tone.** Name the register. "Like a friend at dinner." "Like a hiring manager, direct but kind." "Explain to me like I'm 15, not like I'm 5."

**Scope.** Say what is off-limits. "Do not recommend products." "Only about Chapter 3 — ignore the rest." "If I ask you a medical question, remind me it's outside your lane instead of guessing."

## Boundary language that actually works

Wimpy and strong versions of the same boundary:

- Wimpy: "keep it short" — Strong: "under 120 words."
- Wimpy: "don't be too technical" — Strong: "no jargon. If a term is unavoidable, define it in the same sentence."
- Wimpy: "please don't add extra stuff" — Strong: "answer only what I asked. Do not add tips, disclaimers, or follow-up offers."
- Wimpy: "ask before you go off-script" — Strong: "if you need information you don't have, ask me first. Do not assume."

See the difference? A strong boundary is **checkable**. The AI either did it or did not — no vibes required.

## Boundary recipes for common situations

```
For research:
  Use only what you're confident about. If unsure, say "I'm not sure"
  instead of guessing. Rank claims: /solid, /likely, /uncertain.

For decisions:
  Give exactly 3 options: the safe one, the middle one, the bold one.
  One paragraph each. No recommendation unless I ask.

For emotional support:
  Listen first. Reflect back what you heard in one sentence.
  Do not offer advice until I ask for it.
```

That last one is worth rereading. Boundaries are not just for length — they are how you get **the kind of attention you need**.

## When boundaries go too far

Yes, it can happen:

- **Over-fenced.** Ten boundary lines and no room to move. If you have more rules than the task has steps, cut back to two.
- **Cross-cutting.** "Under 100 words" + "explain everything fully" — pick one; they fight.
- **Punishing the format instead of choosing it.** "No bullet points, no lists, no headers" is complain-speak. "Plain paragraphs" is command-speak.

## The one-line boundary habit

Every prompt, one boundary minimum. Even a tiny one:

```
…and under 150 words.
…answer in one sentence first, details after.
…no jargon.
…end with the 3 questions I should ask [someone professional], not answers.
```
