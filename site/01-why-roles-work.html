AI Companion Roles Guide

Why Vague Asks Get Vague Answers

Picture asking a new coworker to "help with the report." They might research it, rewrite it, email your boss about it, or print a cover page. You meant "read my draft and mark the weak paragraphs." A coworker with real social sense narrows in after a few tries. An AI takes your words almost literally — so the guess it makes is exactly as wide as your words were.

The guesswork loop

A vague ask usually triggers this loop:

  1. You ask something broad: "Help me with my essay."
  2. The AI hedges: "I'd be happy to help! What would you like?" — or worse, picks a job for you and runs with it.
  3. You get two paragraphs of something adjacent to what you wanted.
  4. You correct it: "No, I meant…"
  5. Repeat until one of you gets tired.

Each hop costs time and patience. And halfway through, the conversation has picked up stray assumptions — like extra passengers — that follow you for the rest of the chat.

What actually goes wrong

Under the vagueness there are usually four separate failures. Naming them makes them fixable:

  • No job. "Help me" — with what? Critique? Rewrite? Encouragement? Research?
  • No reader. An essay summary for your professor and one for your little sister are different shapes.
  • No limits. Unbounded answers grow. "Tell me about nursing" gets an encyclopedia when you wanted three bullets.
  • No tone. The AI cannot read your mood. Ask casually, and a formal lecture arrives.

The three-part fix: role, job, boundary

Here is the habit this whole manual exists to teach:

ROLE:      You are my editor.
JOB:       Make my paragraph clearer for a job application.
BOUNDARY:  Under 80 words. Show the rewrite only — no lecture.

Why each part earns its place:

  • Role sets the lens. "Editor" means eyes on your words. "Planner" means eyes on your calendar.
  • Job sets the task. One job, stated plainly. If you cannot state it in one sentence, you have not decided it yet — and that is an important discovery, not a failure.
  • Boundary sets the shape. Length, format, what to skip. Boundaries are how you keep the answer human-sized.

A worked example

Vague:

help me open a savings account, i don't know anything about money

What comes back is generally a wall of generic finance text — technically fine, emotionally useless, and (importantly) careful to remind you it is not financial advice, which is fair.

Same need, with the three parts:

You are my plain-English explainer. I have never had a savings account.
Explain the 5 words I'll see most often (APY, minimum balance, withdrawal
limit, FDIC, interest) like I'm your friend at dinner. One sentence each.
Then tell me the 3 questions to ask the bank. Do not recommend specific
banks — I'll bring my own options to you.

Now the role is fixed ("plain-English explainer"), the job is one concrete task ("define 5 words + 3 questions"), and the boundary stops the drift ("no bank recommendations, dinner-table tone"). The answer gets shorter, warmer, and yours.

A metaphor worth keeping

The three parts are like ordering at a diner counter: role is picking the station (grill, salad bar, coffee), job is your order, boundary is "no onions, on one plate." Skip all three and the cook invents a meal.
